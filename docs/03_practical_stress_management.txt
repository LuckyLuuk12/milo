DOCUMENT: Practical stress-management techniques
TOPICS: coping, grounding, relaxation, routines, movement, social support

Acknowledge the student's experience without minimising it. Ask whether they want to explore the cause, try a short exercise, or identify a practical next step.

1. Name the source. Describe the stressor precisely. Identifying a deadline, conflict, uncertainty, financial concern, or lack of sleep makes it easier to consider possible actions. Writing worries down can help organise them.
2. Separate what can be influenced. List what may be changeable and what cannot be controlled right now. Choose one small action from the first list. For issues outside the student's control, focus on support or adapting expectations.
3. Grounding. When overwhelmed, invite the student to put both feet on the floor, notice the support of the chair or ground, look around and name a few things they can see, and notice sounds. Keep this optional. If focusing on breathing or the body makes the person feel worse, stop and try another approach.
4. Make room for thoughts. A thought is not automatically a fact or instruction. A student can notice "I am going to fail" as a thought without having to prove it true or fight it immediately. Encourage a non-judgemental stance.
5. Restore basic routines. Regular sleep and wake times, meals, movement, and small daily activities can provide structure. Suggest small realistic changes, not a complete lifestyle overhaul.
6. Move and spend time outside. A walk, gentle movement, cycling, or another suitable activity may help create a break from studying. Adapt suggestions to the person's abilities and circumstances.
7. Connect with someone. A trusted friend, family member, classmate, mentor, or professional may reduce isolation and help identify options. Ask whether the student wants listening, practical help, or help contacting someone.
8. Choose one manageable next step: open the assignment and write a heading; send one question to a lecturer; take a short walk; eat something; message a trusted person; or make an appointment.

Do not promise that an exercise will remove stress. Techniques affect people differently and do not replace professional help when needed.
